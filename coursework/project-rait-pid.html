Drone Control (PID)

Coursework / RAIT / Drone Control (PID)

What I Did

This project is a simulated dual-rotor drone that starts on the ground with its rotors off and has to fly to a target elevation, move to a target horizontal position, and hold a steady hover there. I wrote the PID controllers that fly it. The vertical axis is the straightforward one: rotor RPM produces thrust, and the drone climbs, hovers or descends depending on how that thrust compares to its weight. The horizontal axis is indirect - the drone cannot move sideways on demand, it has to roll, which is induced by running the two rotors at different thrusts, and then ride the horizontal component of its own thrust across. Both controllers return a change in their actuator each timestep rather than an absolute setting. I also implemented twiddle, a coordinate-ascent search, so the gain values are found automatically by repeatedly flying the simulation rather than dialled in by hand.

What I Learned

The three terms answer three genuinely different questions about the error, and once I framed them that way the controller stopped being a formula to memorize. Proportional asks how far off am I, and drives toward the target - but on anything with momentum it always overshoots, because it is still pushing right up until the moment the error hits zero, by which point the drone is moving. Derivative asks how fast is the error closing, and it is the term that lets the controller start easing off before it arrives. Integral asks how long have I been wrong, and it exists for a failure the other two cannot even see: with a persistent disturbance, like rotors that quietly lose lift or an unexpected payload, the drone settles at a point where the proportional push exactly balances the disturbance, sitting short of the target forever with a small stable error. Only the accumulated history of that error can close the gap.

Integral wind-up was the richest thing in the project, and it taught me more about control than the rest combined. The rotors can only change RPM so fast, so when the controller demands more than that, the extra command is simply discarded - the actuator is saturated and the drone is already doing everything it can. The integral term, though, does not know that. It keeps accumulating error it is physically unable to act on, and by the time the drone finally reaches the target that stored quantity has grown large and keeps commanding motion in the same direction, so the drone sails past the target and only then begins to unwind. The fix is conceptually simple - the controller has to know when it is saturated and stop integrating while it is - but the underlying lesson generalizes well beyond drones: a controller that models its actuator as unlimited is controlling a machine that does not exist, and it will be surprised at exactly the moments that matter.

The two controllers are also coupled, which I did not appreciate until I had them both running. Rolling to move sideways tilts the thrust vector away from vertical, so the same rotor output now supports less of the drone's weight and it sags while it translates. The thrust controller then sees an elevation error that the roll controller created, corrects for it, and changes the very quantity the roll controller is using to move. Tuning each one in isolation on its own axis produces gains that look excellent alone and misbehave together. That was a useful correction to my instinct to decompose a problem into independent pieces - the decomposition is only valid if the pieces are actually independent, and here the physics says they are not.

Twiddle was my first real experience of automated parameter search, and it beat my intuition comfortably - it explores combinations I would never have tried by hand and it does not get attached to a gain it picked earlier. But it optimizes exactly the error number I hand it and nothing else. If that number says nothing about how violently the drone got to the target, the search will happily return gains that look great by the metric and fly badly by eye. Choosing what to measure ended up being a more consequential decision than choosing how to search, which is the same lesson I keep meeting in machine learning under a different name. It is also a local search, so where I start it and how big the first steps are shape which solution it finds at all.
