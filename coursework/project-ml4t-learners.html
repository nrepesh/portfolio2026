Assess Learners

Coursework / ML4T / Assess Learners

What I Did

I built four supervised learners from scratch in pure Python: a Decision Tree (DT), a Random Tree (RT), a Bagging ensemble, and a Linear Regression baseline. I then designed three controlled experiments to understand their behaviors: measuring overfitting as leaf size shrinks, testing whether bagging reduces overfitting, and comparing training time and predictive accuracy between DT and RT. All experiments ran on the Istanbul dataset using a 60/40 train-test split.

Key Results

Experiment Finding
Overfitting vs leaf size At leaf_size=1, test RMSE was 0.0078 vs training RMSE of 0.0. Gap reduced to near-zero at leaf_size=6.
Bagging reduces overfitting? Yes, but not eliminated. DT bag ensemble test RMSE dropped to 0.0060 (from 0.0078). RT bag ensemble: 0.0064.
DT vs RT speed RT trained ~12x faster (0.025s vs 0.303s at leaf_size=1).
DT vs RT accuracy DT slightly higher R-squared (0.524 vs 0.488) due to correlated feature selection.

What I Learned

Building a Decision Tree from the ground up was the most instructive coding exercise of the term. Until you actually write the recursive split logic, you don't truly appreciate why leaf_size is the single most important hyperparameter. At leaf_size=1, the tree memorizes every data point. The training RMSE hits zero, and the model becomes useless outside the training set. Watching that gap shrink as I increased leaf_size was the clearest "aha" moment so far.

Bagging was fascinating. The intuition is simple: average many noisy models and the noise cancels out. But the reality is more nuanced. Bagging with Decision Trees helps modestly because the trees are correlated (they all pick the same best feature first). Bagging with Random Trees helps more because the trees are decorrelated (they pick random features). The ensemble of 20 Random Trees achieved competitive accuracy while being dramatically faster to train.

The speed difference between DT and RT was counterintuitive at first. DT is slower because it's exhaustively searching for the best feature at every split. RT is faster because it just picks a feature at random. The trade-off is that individual RTs are weaker learners, but that weakness is exactly why ensembles of them work so well. Random Forests (which this project essentially builds the foundation for) exploit this beautifully.
